            The page starts in January 2025, when LAPD's new records system is complete. LAPD's earlier crime records count differently (one crime per report, where the new ones count every offense), and 2024 is split between the two, so earlier years can't be set beside these. These are crashes and police reports, not a count of everyone who walks or of everything that happens. Busy places have more of both partly because more people are there; nothing here is adjusted for foot traffic. Police reports also depend on where police patrol and on who calls.

            Method How this was measured

            How this was measured, and what it can't tell you

            The circle

            The card counts what's within a distance of the spot you pick: 100 to 500 m, 200 m to start (about 650 ft, a 3-minute walk). Distances are as the crow flies, not along the streets. Police reports count at the place LAPD gives them, and crashes where the page places them. The card names the spot by the nearest intersection, on the street you picked where it can. The ranks compare the circle with one the same size around each of the City's intersections (where two or more streets meet): “Top 4% of LA intersections” means only 4% have as many or more; 96% have fewer.

            Dates

            The dates menu picks which police reports, calls and crashes the map and the card count: everything since January 2025, the last 12 months, the last 90 days, the last full calendar month, or the last 30 days. Each is counted back from that dataset's own latest day, since each runs behind by a different amount: LAPD's reports about two weeks, its calls about a week, and the state's crash reports weeks to months, so recent dates have fewer crashes than they will. The ranks compare whole windows at any time of day, so they show only for all the dates. The summary below the map always covers all of them.

            Police reports

            LAPD's crime reports on data.lacity.org from January 2025, when LAPD's new records system is complete, grouped into robbery (including carjacking); assault and other violence (assault, threats, murder, rape and other sex offenses); pickpocketing and purse snatching; weapons; drug offenses; and car break-ins (thefts from a vehicle). A report counts once per group. Only reports in public places count: streets, sidewalks, alleys, parks, parking lots, buses, trains and stations, stores, restaurants and other places open to the public, and homeless encampments. Reports in homes, workplaces, schools, hospitals and hotels aren't counted, nor ones whose place is given only as a vehicle, nor reports of domestic violence. LAPD rounds each report's place to its hundred block or intersection, so the map shows those points, not the exact spot. Where LAPD doesn't know the time, it writes midnight or noon; those reports count, but not in the times of day or in daylight and after dark. The map shades neighborhoods by reports of violence and robbery per km of street.

            Calls to police

            LAPD's calls for service from January 2025, grouped by LAPD's radio codes: fights and assaults (battery, assault with a weapon other than a gun, and disturbance calls about a fight or an assault); someone with a gun or knife (shots fired or heard, and disturbance calls about someone with a gun or a knife); robbery (carjacking and purse snatching included); and threats and harassment (criminal threats and indecent exposure). Calls marked domestic violence aren't counted. LAPD gives each call only its reporting district, not where in it, so the card counts the calls in the spot's district, not the circle, and outlines the district on the map; the layer shades each district by these calls per km². The rank compares the district's calls per km² with every other district's. A call's time is when police were sent. A call is what someone reported, not what police found; one event can bring several calls, and a call can come from anywhere, homes included.

            People walking hit

            California's crash reports (the California Crash Reporting System) from January 2025: each person walking who was hurt or killed by a vehicle in the City of Los Angeles, as the report lists them, whether LAPD or another agency took the report. Crashes on freeways aren't counted. LAPD's reports give two streets, not the spot (“Vermont Ave at 8th St, 50 ft west”), so the page places each crash where the two streets meet, then that far along the first street, or at the house number when the report gives one. Where the same two streets meet in more than one place, LAPD's reporting district picks. The main cause is the report's primary collision factor, in plain words. How badly each person was hurt isn't shown on the card: a circle holds so few people that it could point to someone.

            High Injury Network

            The High Injury Network for people walking from LADOT's 2024 Vision Zero Safety Study. LADOT scored each street by the crashes there from 2017 to 2021 in which someone was hurt, counting deaths and severe injuries most, and people walking, children and people over 65 more; and by what the street is like: fast or busy traffic, truck routes, schools and bus stops nearby, many people walking, and neighborhoods the City counts as in the most need. The network is the 551 miles that scored highest for people walking: 7.5% of the City's streets, with 72% of the crashes that killed or badly hurt someone walking in those years. The map marks a block as on it when most of the block lies within 20 m of LADOT's line for that street. The card names the streets on it that pass through the circle. The share of people walking hit on it, under the map, counts a crash as on it when the page placed it on one of its blocks or at one of their corners.

            After dark

            Between sunset and sunrise in Los Angeles on that date, worked out from the date to within a few minutes of the published times. Dusk after sunset and dawn before sunrise count as after dark. A police report or a crash goes by the time it happened, as the report gives it.

            Streets

            Each line on the map is one stretch of street from one intersection to the next, from the City of Los Angeles street centerlines, named by its hundred block from the house numbers along it. Freeways aren't in the centerlines. The page covers the City of Los Angeles, where LAPD takes the police reports; cities inside or next to it, like Santa Monica, Beverly Hills and Culver City, have their own police.

            Limits

            Police reports show where reports were taken, which depends on where police patrol and on who calls or files online, as much as on where things happen. A street with few reports may just have few people on it; counts aren't adjusted for foot traffic. Calls depend on who calls, and how readily, as much as on what happens. Drug offenses are reports of having or selling drugs; they don't count overdoses. LAPD's reports run about two weeks behind. Crash reports reach the state weeks or months late (half within a month, most within three), so the last few months of people walking hit are still filling in; and a crash nobody reported isn't counted.
